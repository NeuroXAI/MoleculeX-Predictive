\begin{abstract}
We present MoleculeX-Predictive, a knowledge-augmented transformer framework for explainable molecular property prediction. The system fine-tunes ChemBERTa on 16,847 ChEMBL compounds, concatenating learned SMILES embeddings with five RDKit physicochemical descriptors through a two-layer MLP to jointly predict pIC50, logP, and atom count. Under scaffold-based cross-validation the model achieves $R^2 = 0.918 \pm 0.012$ for pIC50 (MAE = 0.152), $R^2 = 0.867 \pm 0.015$ for logP, and $R^2 = 0.991 \pm 0.003$ for atom count, outperforming a standard ChemBERTa baseline by 10.5\% on pIC50 and a Random Forest trained on the same descriptor set by 40.4\%. To interpret these predictions we integrate three complementary XAI methods---SHAP, LIME, and Integrated Gradients---and show that their top-feature rankings correlate at $r = 0.68$--$0.73$, with 78\% three-way agreement on the most influential descriptors. Across 200 test molecules the explanations identify atoms belonging to known pharmacophore motifs in 85\% of cases. These results demonstrate that augmenting a chemical-language model with domain knowledge can improve both predictive accuracy and the chemical interpretability of the resulting explanations.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The intersection of artificial intelligence and molecular science has revolutionized drug discovery and materials research, enabling powerful new capabilities in predicting molecular properties and understanding structure-activity relationships \cite{chithrananda2020chemberta,polykovskiy2020molecular}. However, the complexity of modern deep learning models, particularly transformer-based architectures, has created a significant challenge: while these models achieve remarkable predictive accuracy, their decision-making processes remain largely opaque, hindering trust and adoption in high-stakes pharmaceutical and chemical applications \cite{arrieta2020explainable,gunning2019darpa}.

The pharmaceutical industry faces increasing pressure to develop more efficient drug discovery pipelines while maintaining rigorous safety and efficacy standards \cite{tiwari2023applications}. Traditional approaches to molecular property prediction often rely on hand-crafted descriptors and linear models that, while interpretable, fail to capture the complex non-linear relationships inherent in molecular systems \cite{rogers2010extended}. Conversely, modern deep learning approaches, including graph neural networks and transformer models, excel at learning intricate patterns but operate as "black boxes," making it difficult for chemists and regulatory bodies to understand and validate their predictions \cite{samek2017explainable}.

SMILES (Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System) notation has emerged as the de facto standard for representing molecular structures in computational chemistry due to its compact, text-like format that enables direct application of natural language processing techniques \cite{weininger1988smiles}. The development of ChemBERTa, a transformer model pre-trained on millions of chemical structures, has demonstrated the potential of applying advanced NLP architectures to molecular property prediction tasks \cite{chithrananda2020chemberta}. However, the lack of interpretability in such models remains a critical barrier to their widespread adoption in drug discovery workflows.

Explainable AI (XAI) has emerged as a crucial field addressing the interpretability challenge in machine learning \cite{arrieta2020explainable,lundberg2017shap}. Several techniques have gained prominence, including SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) for global feature importance analysis \cite{lundberg2017shap}, LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) for local prediction understanding \cite{ribeiro2016lime}, and Integrated Gradients for attribution analysis in neural networks \cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic}. However, the application of these techniques to molecular property prediction presents unique challenges, particularly in handling the sequential nature of SMILES representations and the integration of domain-specific chemical knowledge.

Current state of the art research reveals several critical gaps in the integration of explainable AI with molecular property prediction. First, most existing work focuses on individual explanation methods rather than comprehensive multi-modal approaches. Our framework addresses this by integrating SHAP, LIME, and Integrated Gradients to provide both global and local insights into model behavior. Second, the application of XAI techniques to transformer-based models in chemistry remains limited, particularly for complex input spaces that combine SMILES sequences with chemical descriptors. Our work specifically addresses this challenge through custom wrappers and hybrid input handling.

In this work, we present MoleculeX-Predictive, a comprehensive framework that addresses these challenges by combining the predictive power of ChemBERTa with multiple XAI techniques to create a transparent, accurate, and practical system for molecular property prediction. Our approach integrates transformer-based learning with knowledge-augmented features derived from established chemical descriptors, providing both high accuracy and interpretable insights into the factors driving molecular properties. We demonstrate the potential of our framework through producing property predictions that were not possible with previous frameworks. 

Most existing XAI implementations are designed for individual predictions or small datasets, limiting their applicability to industrial-scale molecular screening workflows. Our prototype put these concerns into consideration. Previous work has not adequately exploited fine tuning of transformer-based models. We implement this through the integration of domain-specific chemical knowledge with transformer models for enhanced interpretability. Our knowledge-augmented architecture provides explicit chemical information that complements learned representations, resulting in more interpretable predictions.

The key \textbf{research} contributions of this work include:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A knowledge-augmented ChemBERTa architecture that concatenates transformer embeddings with five RDKit physicochemical descriptors through a two-layer MLP, yielding $R^2 = 0.918$ for pIC50 under scaffold-based cross-validation---a 10.5\% improvement over the standard ChemBERTa baseline and a 40.4\% improvement over a Random Forest trained on the same descriptor set.
\item A multi-modal XAI framework integrating SHAP, LIME, and Integrated Gradients for molecular property prediction, with a quantitative consistency analysis showing $r = 0.68$--$0.73$ inter-method correlation and 85\% pharmacophore identification accuracy.
\item An ablation study isolating the contributions of transformer embeddings, RDKit descriptors, and the knowledge-fusion layer, together with a single-task versus multi-task comparison.
\end{enumerate}

In addition, we provide \textbf{engineering} contributions including a Flask-based REST API with CSV-based explanation caching (95\% reduction in repeated computation) and asynchronous task management, and a Next.js web frontend for interactive molecular visualization and XAI exploration. These engineering components are described for completeness but are not the primary focus of this paper.

\subsection*{System Overview}

MoleculeX-Predictive is organized into three layers. The \textbf{AI engine} comprises the knowledge-augmented ChemBERTa model and the multi-modal XAI module described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. A \textbf{Flask backend} exposes the engine through RESTful endpoints for training, prediction, and explanation generation, and manages CSV-based caching and background-threaded computation. A \textbf{Next.js frontend} provides interactive SMILES input, 2D/3D molecular visualization, and real-time XAI result exploration. Communication flows from the frontend through the Flask API to the AI engine and back, with cached explanations served directly when available. This architecture allows each layer to be developed and scaled independently.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Explainable AI in Molecular Property Prediction}

The application of explainable AI (XAI) techniques to molecular property prediction has gained significant attention as the complexity of deep learning models has increased. Traditional approaches to molecular property prediction often relied on hand-crafted descriptors and linear models that, while interpretable, failed to capture the complex non-linear relationships inherent in molecular systems \cite{rogers2010extended}. Conversely, modern deep learning approaches, including graph neural networks and transformer models, excel at learning intricate patterns but operate as "black boxes," making it difficult for chemists and regulatory bodies to understand and validate their predictions \cite{samek2017explainable}.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) has emerged as one of the most theoretically grounded approaches to model explanation in molecular property prediction \cite{lundberg2017shap}. Recent studies have demonstrated its effectiveness in identifying key molecular substructures that influence biological activity. For instance, research on pIC50 prediction using deep learning algorithms has shown that SHAP can successfully identify pharmacophore features and functional groups that contribute to binding affinity \cite{researchgate_pic50_2024}. The method's ability to provide both local and global explanations makes it particularly valuable for understanding complex structure-activity relationships.

LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) offers a complementary approach by focusing on local explanations through the training of interpretable surrogate models in the neighborhood of specific predictions \cite{ribeiro2016lime}. In molecular property prediction, LIME has proven particularly effective for understanding individual compound predictions and identifying potential failure modes in complex models. Studies on fine-tuning transformer models for bioactivity prediction have demonstrated LIME's utility in providing actionable insights for medicinal chemistry applications \cite{imani_alzheimer_2024}.

Previous approaches to molecular model interpretability have primarily relied on descriptor-based interpretations and counterfactual explanations. Wellawatte et al. introduced Molecular Model Agnostic Counterfactual Explanations (MMACE), which generates counterfactuals to explain model predictions \cite{wellawatte2022mmace}. While counterfactual explanations offer valuable perspectives, they require generating chemically valid counterfactuals, which can be computationally intensive and may not always provide chemically meaningful insights.

In contrast, SHAP and LIME provide direct feature attribution without necessitating the generation of new molecular structures, making them more efficient for large-scale applications. Our multi-modal approach combining SHAP, LIME, and Integrated Gradients addresses the limitations of single-method explanations by providing multiple perspectives on model decision-making, ensuring comprehensive interpretability for molecular property prediction tasks.

\subsubsection*{Cross-Domain XAI in High-Stakes Applications (2025)}

The integration of explainability into safety-critical AI systems is a rapidly growing trend that extends well beyond drug discovery. Chowdhury et al.\ \cite{chowdhury2025ensemble} proposed an ensemble ANN approach with advanced XAI for liver-disease identification, demonstrating that optimal-feature selection guided by SHAP and LIME significantly improves diagnostic performance---an approach conceptually analogous to our use of SHAP-guided feature analysis for molecular property drivers. Hussain et al.\ \cite{hussain2025neuroblend} introduced NeuroBlend-3, a hybrid deep/machine-learning framework with XAI for multi-class brain-tumor detection from MRI scans. Their combination of a deep feature extractor with a traditional classifier mirrors our hybrid Transformer/Knowledge-layer design, and their use of Grad-CAM for spatial attribution parallels our use of Integrated Gradients for token-level attribution in SMILES. \subsection{Transformer Models in Chemical Informatics}

The application of transformer architectures to chemical informatics represents a significant paradigm shift from traditional descriptor-based approaches. The foundational "Attention is All You Need" paper \cite{vaswani2017attention} introduced the transformer architecture that revolutionized natural language processing and has since been adapted for molecular property prediction.

ChemBERTa, developed by Chithrananda et al., demonstrated the effectiveness of adapting BERT's pre-training methodology to chemical data, achieving state-of-the-art performance on various molecular property prediction benchmarks \cite{chithrananda2020chemberta}. The model builds upon the foundational BERT architecture \cite{devlin2018bert} and leverages pre-training strategies similar to those used in language models \cite{radford2019language,brown2020language,liu2019roberta,clark2020electra,lan2019albert}. The model's ability to learn contextual representations of molecular substructures without explicit feature engineering has made it particularly attractive for complex property prediction tasks.

%\subsubsection{Comparison with Other Deep Learning Approaches}

Recent studies have compared transformer models with other deep learning architectures for molecular property prediction. Research on predicting pIC50 using deep learning algorithms has shown that transformer-based approaches consistently outperform traditional methods \cite{researchgate_pic50_2024}. When compared to other deep learning models:

\textbf{Graph Neural Networks (GNNs):} While GNNs excel at capturing molecular topology through graph representations \cite{duvenaud2015convolutional,kearnes2016molecular,velivckovic2017graph,kipf2016semi,hamilton2017inductive,scarselli2008graph}, transformer models demonstrate superior performance in learning sequential patterns within SMILES representations. Our knowledge-augmented ChemBERTa approach combines the strengths of both paradigms by integrating graph-based chemical descriptors with transformer embeddings.

\textbf{Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs):} Traditional CNNs applied to molecular fingerprints show limited ability to capture long-range dependencies in molecular structures \cite{duvenaud2015convolutional,he2016deep,simonyan2014very,szegedy2015going}. Transformer models, with their self-attention mechanisms, can effectively model relationships between distant atoms in molecular sequences, providing more comprehensive molecular understanding.

\textbf{Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs):} RNNs have been applied to SMILES sequences but suffer from vanishing gradient problems and limited parallelization capabilities \cite{liu2021molecular}. The transformer architecture addresses these limitations through parallel processing and attention mechanisms, enabling more efficient training and better performance on molecular property prediction tasks.


\textbf{Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)}: GNNs directly operate on molecular graphs and can capture structural information effectively \cite{gao2022graph,duvenaud2015convolutional}. However, they often require significant computational resources and may not scale well to large molecular datasets \cite{kearnes2016molecular,chen2020graph}. Recent advances in graph neural network pre-training strategies \cite{hu2019strategies} have improved their performance, with studies reporting R² scores of 0.82-0.85 for pIC50 prediction using GNNs \cite{wu2018moleculenet}.

\textbf{Transformer Models}: Our knowledge-augmented ChemBERTa approach achieves $R^2$ = 0.92 for pIC50 prediction, demonstrating superior performance compared to these alternative architectures. The self-attention mechanisms in transformers enable the capture of complex, non-linear relationships within molecular data, leading to more accurate predictions.

\subsection{Knowledge-Augmented vs. Standard Transformer Models}

The integration of domain-specific knowledge into transformer models represents a significant advancement in molecular property prediction. Standard transformer models without knowledge augmentation rely solely on learned representations from pre-training, which may not capture all relevant chemical principles. Studies comparing knowledge-augmented approaches with standard transformer models have shown consistent improvements in predictive performance.

Our approach integrates RDKit-derived chemical descriptors with ChemBERTa embeddings, achieving superior performance compared to standard ChemBERTa implementations. The knowledge integration layer provides explicit chemical information that complements learned representations, resulting in more accurate and interpretable predictions. This hybrid approach addresses the limitations of purely data-driven methods while maintaining the representational power of transformer architectures.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}



\subsection{System Architecture Overview}

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:complete_workflow}, MoleculeX-Predictive implements a multi-layered architecture designed to seamlessly integrate predictive modeling with comprehensive explainability. The system comprises four primary components: (1) a knowledge-augmented ChemBERTa model for property prediction, (2) a multi-modal XAI engine implementing SHAP, LIME, and Integrated Gradients, (3) a Flask-based backend API for model orchestration and data management, and (4) a Next.js frontend providing interactive visualization and user interaction capabilities.

The architecture follows a microservices approach, enabling independent scaling and optimization of each component. This design facilitates deployment flexibility and allows for easy integration of additional XAI techniques or predictive models as they become available.



\subsection{Knowledge-Augmented ChemBERTa Model}

\subsubsection*{Base Architecture}

Our predictive model builds upon the ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1 architecture, which has been pre-trained on approximately 77 million SMILES strings from the ZINC database \cite{chithrananda2020chemberta}. The base model employs a transformer architecture with 6 attention heads, 12 hidden layers, and a hidden size of 768 dimensions, optimized specifically for chemical sequence understanding. This architecture leverages the proven effectiveness of transformer models in processing sequential data, adapted specifically for molecular SMILES representations.

The model architecture is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{\text{base}} = \text{ChemBERTa}(\mathbf{s})
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{s}$ represents the tokenized SMILES sequence and $\mathbf{h}_{\text{base}}$ is the resulting contextualized embedding.

\subsubsection*{Knowledge Integration Layer}

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:knowledge_architecture}, we augment the base ChemBERTa embeddings with molecular descriptors calculated using RDKit \cite{landrum2013rdkit} to incorporate domain-specific chemical knowledge.

\textbf{Rationale for knowledge augmentation.}
ChemBERTa learns contextual token representations from SMILES strings, but its pre-training objective (masked-language modelling on ZINC) does not explicitly encode global physicochemical quantities such as lipophilicity or hydrogen-bond capacity. RDKit descriptors provide this complementary signal directly. We verified the complementarity empirically: the Pearson correlation between the [CLS] embedding norm and each RDKit descriptor is $|r| < 0.25$ across the training set, confirming that the two representation spaces carry largely non-redundant information.

\textbf{Potential information overlap.}
We acknowledge that some degree of overlap exists between the RDKit descriptors (e.g., LogP) and the predicted targets (logP). However, the knowledge layer does not feed target values to the prediction heads; it feeds \emph{input-side} physicochemical properties that are computable from the molecular graph \emph{before} any prediction is made. Moreover, the ablation study in Section~\ref{sec:results} confirms that removing the knowledge layer degrades performance across all tasks, indicating that these features provide information beyond what the transformer alone captures.

\textbf{Fusion design choice.}
We concatenate the [CLS] embedding ($\mathbb{R}^{768}$) with the 64-dimensional knowledge vector and pass the result through task-specific linear heads. We chose concatenation followed by an MLP over alternatives (additive fusion, cross-attention, gating) for three reasons: (i) it preserves the full information from both streams without destructive interference, (ii) it adds minimal parameters ($\approx$5\,k per head), keeping the model lightweight and reducing overfitting risk on our 16,847-compound dataset, and (iii) it yields the best validation $R^2$ in preliminary experiments (concatenation+MLP: 0.918; additive: 0.891; gating: 0.905).

The knowledge features include:

\begin{itemize}
\item Molecular weight (MolWt)
\item Number of hydrogen bond donors (NumHDonors)
\item Number of hydrogen bond acceptors (NumHAcceptors)
\item Lipophilicity (LogP)
\item Number of rotatable bonds (NumRotatableBonds)
\end{itemize}

The knowledge integration is performed through a dedicated neural network:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{\text{knowledge}} = \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_2 \cdot \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1 \cdot \mathbf{f}_{\text{chemical}} + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2)
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{f}_{\text{chemical}}$ represents the chemical feature vector, and $\mathbf{W}_1, \mathbf{W}_2, \mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2$ are learnable parameters.

\subsubsection*{Multi-Task Prediction Heads}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/figure2.pdf}
\caption{Knowledge-Augmented ChemBERTa model architecture showing (a) SMILES tokenization and embedding, (b) chemical descriptor integration, and (c) multi-task prediction heads.}
\label{fig:knowledge_architecture}
\end{figure}

The combined representation is used for multi-task prediction of three key molecular properties:

\begin{align}
\mathbf{h}_{\text{combined}} &= [\mathbf{h}_{\text{base}}[0]; \mathbf{h}_{\text{knowledge}}] \\
\hat{y}_{\text{pIC50}} &= \mathbf{W}_{\text{pIC50}} \cdot \mathbf{h}_{\text{combined}} + b_{\text{pIC50}} \\
\hat{y}_{\text{logP}} &= \mathbf{W}_{\text{logP}} \cdot \mathbf{h}_{\text{combined}} + b_{\text{logP}} \\
\hat{y}_{\text{atoms}} &= \mathbf{W}_{\text{atoms}} \cdot \mathbf{h}_{\text{combined}} + b_{\text{atoms}}
\end{align}

where $[;]$ denotes concatenation, $\mathbf{h}_{\text{base}}[0]$ represents the [CLS] token embedding, and each prediction head has dedicated weight matrices and biases.



\subsection{Multi-Modal XAI Framework}

Our multi-modal XAI approach integrates three complementary explanation techniques to provide comprehensive insights into model behavior. The term "multi-modal" refers to the combination of different explanation modalities: global feature importance (SHAP), local prediction understanding (LIME), and gradient-based attribution (Integrated Gradients). This approach addresses the limitations of single-method explanations by providing multiple perspectives on model decision-making.

The integration of multiple XAI techniques is particularly important for molecular property prediction because different explanation methods reveal different aspects of the model's reasoning process. SHAP provides global insights into which features are most important across the entire dataset, LIME offers local explanations for individual predictions, and Integrated Gradients reveals how specific input components contribute to predictions through gradient analysis.

\subsubsection*{SHAP Implementation}

We implement SHAP explanations using KernelExplainer for model-agnostic analysis. The implementation addresses the dual nature of our input space (discrete SMILES tokens and continuous chemical features) through a unified perturbation strategy:

\begin{equation}
\phi_j = \frac{1}{|S|!} \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{j\}} |S|!(|N|-|S|-1)![f(S \cup \{j\}) - f(S)]
\end{equation}

where $\phi_j$ represents the SHAP value for feature $j$, $N$ is the set of all features, $S$ is a subset of features, and $f$ is the model prediction function.

For computational efficiency, we employ k-means clustering to create representative background samples:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_{\text{background}} = \text{k-means}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{train}}, k=50)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{LIME Integration}

LIME explanations focus on local interpretability through surrogate model training. We adapt LIME for our hybrid input space by creating perturbations that respect both the sequential nature of SMILES and the continuous nature of chemical descriptors:

\begin{equation}
\xi(x) = \arg\min_{g \in G} L(f, g, \pi_x) + \Omega(g)
\end{equation}

where $\xi(x)$ is the explanation for instance $x$, $G$ is the class of interpretable models, $L$ is the locality-aware loss function, $\pi_x$ defines the local neighborhood, and $\Omega(g)$ penalizes model complexity.

\subsubsection*{Integrated Gradients}

Integrated Gradients is a gradient-based attribution method that provides explanations by integrating gradients along a path from a baseline input to the actual input \cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic}. This method satisfies several desirable properties including sensitivity, implementation invariance, and completeness, making it particularly suitable for neural network explanations.

For our knowledge-augmented ChemBERTa model, we implement a custom wrapper that handles the embedding layer to enable gradient-based attribution:

\begin{equation}
\text{IG}_i(x) = (x_i - x'_i) \times \int_{\alpha=0}^{1} \frac{\partial F(x' + \alpha(x - x'))}{\partial x_i} d\alpha
\end{equation}

where $x'$ represents the baseline, $x$ is the input, and $F$ is the model function. The integral is approximated using numerical integration with 50 steps for computational efficiency.

The baseline selection for Integrated Gradients is a critical design choice that significantly impacts attribution quality and interpretability. We compared three candidate baselines:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Zero baseline} (all-zero token embeddings): represents the absence of molecular information---the null state before any molecular structure is introduced.
\item \textbf{Mean baseline} (average embedding across all training-set tokens): represents the ``average SMILES context'' but may introduce bias from the training distribution.
\item \textbf{Random baseline} (uniformly sampled embedding vectors): theoretically unbiased but yields unstable attributions across runs.
\end{enumerate}

We evaluated each baseline on 200 test compounds using three criteria: (i)~\emph{convergence delta} (lower is better), (ii)~\emph{attribution stability} (coefficient of variation across 10 repeated runs), and (iii)~\emph{chemical plausibility} (percentage of top-3 attributed tokens corresponding to pharmacophore-relevant atoms as judged by a medicinal chemist). The zero baseline achieved the best overall profile: convergence delta $= 0.0032 \pm 0.0011$, CV $< 2\%$, and 83\% chemical plausibility. The mean baseline yielded comparable convergence ($0.0041$) but lower chemical plausibility (71\%), because attributions were biased toward rare tokens that deviate from the mean. The random baseline showed unacceptably high CV ($>15\%$) and was discarded. Our implementation uses \texttt{baseline = torch.zeros\_like(input\_embeddings)} with a convergence threshold of 0.01 and 50 integration steps, which proved sufficient for convergence.

Attribution stability analysis confirmed the reliability of our Integrated Gradients implementation. The mean convergence delta across all test molecules was 0.0032 ± 0.0011, well below the threshold of 0.01. To validate stability, we repeated Integrated Gradients computation (n=10) on the same molecule and found that the coefficient of variation (CV) for attributions was less than 2\% for all tokens, with maximum attribution variance of 0.0008 (negligible). These results demonstrate that Integrated Gradients attributions are stable and reproducible, providing confidence in the reliability of our token-level explanations.

The implementation uses a custom wrapper class that enables gradient computation through the embedding layer, allowing us to compute attributions for individual SMILES tokens while maintaining the model's predictive performance and providing chemically meaningful explanations.

\subsection{Data Processing Pipeline}

\subsubsection*{SMILES Preprocessing}

Input SMILES undergo comprehensive standardization using RDKit to ensure consistency and improve model performance. The preprocessing pipeline addresses several challenges inherent in molecular data representation:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sanitization and validation}: Removes invalid atoms, corrects valence errors, and ensures chemical validity. This step is critical for preventing model errors from malformed structures and maintaining data quality.
\item \textbf{Canonical SMILES generation}: Converts SMILES to canonical form ensuring consistent representation across different input formats. This is essential for proper tokenization and avoiding duplicate representations of the same molecule.
\item \textbf{Descriptor calculation and caching}: Computes RDKit molecular descriptors (MolWt, LogP, NumHDonors, etc.) with intelligent caching for computational efficiency. This provides domain knowledge integration and reduces computational overhead during training and inference.
\item \textbf{Tokenization using ChemBERTa vocabulary}: Converts SMILES to token sequences using the pre-trained ChemBERTa vocabulary. This enables transformer-based processing while maintaining chemical context and structural information.
\end{enumerate}

The preprocessing pipeline is designed to handle the inherent variability in SMILES representations while preserving essential chemical information. Canonicalization ensures that identical molecules are represented consistently, which is crucial for proper model training and evaluation. The integration of RDKit descriptors provides domain-specific knowledge that complements the learned representations from the transformer model.

\subsection{Training}

\subsubsection*{Loss Function}

We employ a multi-task loss function that balances the three prediction objectives:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \alpha \mathcal{L}_{\text{pIC50}} + \beta \mathcal{L}_{\text{logP}} + \gamma \mathcal{L}_{\text{atoms}}
\end{equation}

where each component loss is mean squared error:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{property}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
\end{equation}

\textbf{Loss weight selection.}
The weights $\alpha = 1.0$, $\beta = 0.5$, and $\gamma = 0.1$ were selected through a grid search over $\{0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}$ for each weight, optimizing aggregate validation $R^2$ on the held-out fold. pIC50 receives the highest weight because it is the primary pharmacological endpoint and exhibits the largest dynamic range and prediction difficulty. LogP is weighted at half because its variance is smaller and RDKit already provides a strong analytical approximation. Atom count receives the lowest weight because it is a near-deterministic structural property that the model learns quickly; assigning it a larger weight risks dominating the gradient signal and degrading pIC50 performance. We observed a mild trade-off: increasing $\beta$ from 0.5 to 1.0 improved logP $R^2$ by 0.008 but reduced pIC50 $R^2$ by 0.014, confirming that the chosen weighting strikes a practical balance.

\textbf{Multi-task impact on interpretability.}
The shared transformer backbone means that SHAP and LIME attributions for one target may reflect information sharing across tasks. We address this by computing per-task explanations independently, each using the relevant prediction head. The consistency analysis in Section~\ref{sec:results} confirms that the dominant features for each task are chemically distinct (e.g., H-bond donors for pIC50, LogP descriptors for logP), indicating that the multi-task setup does not blur task-specific attributions.

\subsubsection*{Training Strategy}

The model is trained using the AdamW optimizer with learning rate scheduling:

\begin{equation}
\text{lr}(t) = \text{lr}_{\text{base}} \times \max(0.1, \cos(\frac{\pi t}{T}))
\end{equation}

where $t$ is the current training step and $T$ is the total number of training steps.

\subsection{Scalability Optimizations}


To address the computational overhead of XAI methods in high-throughput scenarios, we implement a CSV-based caching system:

\begin{enumerate}
\item SHAP values are computed once and stored in structured CSV format
\item LIME explanations are cached with timestamps to enable selective recomputation
\item Integrated Gradients results are stored with attribution metadata
\end{enumerate}

 

Long-running computations (training, large-scale prediction, XAI generation) are handled through background threading:

\begin{equation}
\text{Task}(f, \text{args}) \rightarrow \text{ThreadPool.submit}(f, \text{args})
\end{equation}

This ensures responsive user interaction while maintaining computational efficiency.

\subsection{Performance Optimizations}

\subsubsection*{CSV-Based Caching System}

To optimize explanation generation for high-throughput scenarios, we implement a CSV-based caching mechanism. Each explanation result is keyed by the canonical SMILES string, model checkpoint hash, and XAI method identifier. When a prediction or explanation is requested, the cache is consulted first; a cache hit returns the stored result in $<$10\,ms, avoiding re-computation.

\textbf{Caching evaluation protocol.}
We measured cache effectiveness on a simulated screening workflow of 10,000 compounds drawn from ChEMBL with 30\% repeated queries (mimicking iterative lead-optimization cycles). Without caching, total explanation time was 2.3 hours (SHAP + LIME + IG). With caching enabled, total time dropped to 0.34 hours after the initial pass---a 95\% reduction in wall-clock time for repeated compounds. Cache lookup adds negligible overhead ($<$1\,ms per query).

\textbf{Handling model updates.}
The cache key includes a hash of the model checkpoint file. When the model is retrained or fine-tuned, the checkpoint hash changes and all cached explanations are automatically invalidated, ensuring that stale explanations are never served. This design imposes a one-time re-computation cost after each model update.

\textbf{Scalability.}
The system has been tested up to $\sim$100,000 compounds, which represents a large-scale evaluation. The CSV backend has inherent limitations for very large compound libraries; replacing it with an indexed database (e.g., SQLite or Redis) would allow scalability to millions of compounds without API changes, though we have not empirically validated performance at that scale.

\subsubsection*{Asynchronous Task Management}

Long-running computations (training, batch prediction, XAI generation) are managed through Python's \texttt{concurrent.futures.ThreadPoolExecutor} with status tracking, ensuring responsive user interaction while maintaining computational efficiency. The system is designed for scalable deployment with GPU acceleration support and optimized memory management through batch processing and gradient checkpointing.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:eval_protocol}

\textbf{Scaffold split strategy.}
To ensure realistic assessment of model generalization to novel chemical scaffolds, we employed scaffold-based splitting using Bemis--Murcko scaffolds \cite{bemis1996properties} rather than random data splits. Each compound was mapped to its generic Bemis--Murcko scaffold using RDKit's \texttt{MurckoScaffold.MakeScaffoldGeneric()} function. Scaffolds were then sorted by frequency (most common first) and greedily assigned to the training, validation, and test sets with a target ratio of 80/10/10, ensuring that all compounds sharing the same scaffold are placed in the same split. This procedure guarantees zero scaffold overlap between splits. The resulting splits contained 13,478 training compounds (3,374 scaffolds), 1,685 validation compounds (422 scaffolds), and 1,684 test compounds (422 scaffolds).

\textbf{Cross-validation.}
Model performance was evaluated using 5-fold scaffold-based cross-validation. Scaffolds were partitioned into 5 disjoint groups while maintaining scaffold integrity. For each fold we trained on 4 groups and validated on the remaining one. Performance metrics are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation across all folds. For all regression tasks, we report Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$), all with mean $\pm$ SD across folds. Classification accuracy is not reported for pIC50 or logP as these are continuous regression tasks.

\textbf{Interpreting disagreements.}
When the three methods disagree on the most important feature, a chemist should consider the explanation granularity: SHAP and LIME highlight which \emph{physicochemical property} drives a prediction (e.g., H-bond donors), while Integrated Gradients reveals which \emph{molecular substructure} encodes that property (e.g., the --OH group at position 4). Disagreement therefore often reflects complementary rather than contradictory information. In practice, we recommend that chemists first consult SHAP for a global ranking of property drivers, then use LIME to confirm the direction and magnitude for a specific compound, and finally inspect Integrated Gradients to localize the effect to specific atoms or functional groups. Persistent disagreement across all three methods (observed in $<$5\% of test compounds) should flag the prediction for manual review.

\textbf{Do explanations merely echo input features?}
A concern raised in the literature is that XAI methods applied to models with hand-crafted inputs may simply recover input-feature importance without revealing the transformer's internal reasoning. We address this in two ways. First, we note that our SHAP and LIME analyses operate on the five RDKit descriptors that serve as explicit model inputs; for these, the explanations \emph{should} reflect input importance, and their value lies in confirming that the model uses chemically sensible features in the expected direction (e.g., H-bond donors positively correlate with pIC50). Second, Integrated Gradients operates on the raw SMILES token embeddings before any descriptor computation, providing a window into the transformer's learned representations. The IG attributions reveal token-level patterns (e.g., aromatic carbons, heteroatom tokens) that are not directly represented in the five descriptor inputs, demonstrating that the transformer contributes genuine representational capacity beyond the knowledge layer.

\subsubsection*{Chemical Validation Protocol}

We define \emph{chemical validation accuracy} as the fraction of test compounds for which at least one of the top-3 XAI-highlighted atoms belongs to a known pharmacophore motif for the target property. The protocol proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item For each test compound, extract the top-3 atoms by attribution magnitude from each XAI method (SHAP contribution to the relevant descriptor, LIME local weight, IG token attribution mapped back to atoms).
\item Identify ground-truth pharmacophore atoms using a curated list of functional-group SMARTS patterns: hydrogen-bond donors (\texttt{[\#7,\#8;H1,H2]}), acceptors (\texttt{[\#7,\#8]}), aromatic rings, and acidic/basic groups relevant to the predicted property.
\item A compound is scored as a ``hit'' if any top-3 atom overlaps with a pharmacophore atom for that property.
\end{enumerate}

The integration of SHAP, LIME, and Integrated Gradients offers complementary perspectives on model decision-making.

The integration of domain-specific chemical knowledge represents a significant advancement over standard transformer approaches. Our analysis reveals several key benefits of knowledge augmentation:

\textbf{Chemical Interpretability}: The integration of RDKit descriptors provides a foundation for chemical interpretation of model predictions. This addresses a key limitation of purely data-driven approaches that may not align with known chemical principles.

\subsubsection*{Advantages Over Traditional Approaches}

Our framework addresses several critical limitations of existing molecular property prediction methods:

\textbf{Mathematical Model Limitations}: Traditional RDKit-based approaches can only compute certain properties directly from SMILES (e.g., logP, molecular weight) but cannot predict complex biological activities like pIC50. Our framework bridges this gap by learning structure-activity relationships from experimental data, enabling prediction of properties that require sophisticated biological modeling.

\textbf{Deep Learning Interpretability Gap}: While previous deep learning models achieve high accuracy, they operate as "black boxes" without providing insights into their decision-making process. Our multi-modal XAI framework addresses this limitation by providing comprehensive explanations through SHAP, LIME, and Integrated Gradients, making the predictions actionable for medicinal chemists.

\textbf{Scalability and Efficiency}: Traditional approaches often require separate models for different properties and lack efficient explanation generation. Our unified framework provides multi-task prediction with optimized caching and asynchronous processing, enabling real-time analysis of large molecular datasets.

\textbf{Knowledge Integration}: Unlike purely data-driven approaches that may miss important chemical principles, our knowledge-augmented architecture explicitly incorporates established molecular descriptors, ensuring that predictions align with known chemical relationships and providing a foundation for model interpretability.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the model's performance on novel chemical scaffolds outside the training distribution requires further validation. Integration of attention mechanism analysis could provide additional insights into the transformer's internal reasoning processes, potentially revealing more nuanced structure-activity relationships.

Third, the biological activity predictions currently rely on simplified binding affinity estimates. Integration of more sophisticated QSAR models or experimental validation would enhance the system's utility for drug discovery applications.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented MoleculeX-Predictive, a framework that addresses the dual challenges of achieving high predictive accuracy and providing structured interpretability in molecular property prediction. Our knowledge-augmented ChemBERTa architecture demonstrates that integrating domain-specific chemical knowledge with transformer-based learning can yield strong performance ($R^2 = 0.918$ for pIC50 under scaffold splits) while enabling meaningful interpretation through multiple XAI modalities.

The multi-modal explainability framework, incorporating SHAP, LIME, and Integrated Gradients, provides complementary insights into the factors driving molecular property predictions. The three methods achieve moderate-to-strong inter-method agreement ($r = 0.68$--$0.73$) and collectively identify known pharmacophore motifs in 85\% of test compounds, lending confidence that the explanations reflect chemically meaningful patterns rather than artifacts.

The practical implementation, including CSV-based caching (95\% reduction in repeated computation) and asynchronous task management, demonstrates the feasibility of deploying XAI-augmented prediction systems in screening-scale workflows, though these engineering contributions are secondary to the modelling advances.

Several limitations should be noted. The training set covers kinase and GPCR scaffolds from ChEMBL; generalization to structurally distant chemical series (macrocycles, PROTACs) remains untested. The SHAP and LIME explanations operate on aggregate descriptors rather than individual atoms, limiting spatial resolution compared to Integrated Gradients. Finally, the MOSES evaluation uses model-generated pIC50 values without experimental ground truth, which limits the conclusions that can be drawn from that benchmark.

\textbf{Future directions.}
We identify three priorities for future work: (i) extension to ADMET and synthetic-accessibility endpoints with integration of 3D structural information, (ii) development of per-prediction uncertainty quantification (e.g., ensemble-based prediction intervals) to flag unreliable estimates, and (iii) validation on larger and more structurally diverse datasets, including prospective experimental evaluation. More broadly, the emergence of multi-modal XAI frameworks in both drug discovery (this work) and clinical diagnostics \cite{chowdhury2025ensemble,hussain2025neuroblend} highlights the need for \emph{unified XAI evaluation standards} that enable cross-domain benchmarking of explanation quality, an effort we intend to contribute to in subsequent studies.
